What Running a Local Agency with Claude Duet Actually Looks Like

By Jeremiah Svaren

Most mornings, my drive into the office takes about 15 minutes. My phone sits in the cupholder, and I spend that ride answering decisions out loud, one at a time. Yes to that email draft. Hold off on that ad change. Move that task to next week. By the time I park, emails are drafted and tasks are already moving.

That's been my setup with Claude Duet for about a month now.

Most local business owners hear "AI" and picture a little chatbot sitting on their website answering questions about hours. That part is fine. The useful part for me is everything happening behind the counter.

So I wanted to walk through what running my local marketing agency, LeadingLocal, with Duet actually looks like: what a normal day feels like, real work it has handled over the last month, what it won't do without me, and the features I still haven't tried yet.

The problem every local business owner runs into

If you run a local business, you know how fast a week gets away from you. One person wearing way too many hats: client work, sales, invoices, ads, follow-ups, plus whatever side projects you're trying to push forward when things quiet down.

Because you're moving all day, the real work ends up living in two places: your inbox and your head.

You read an email between jobs, tell yourself you'll deal with it after lunch, get pulled into two other things, and by Thursday it's buried 40 messages down. Nothing gets tracked properly, so things slip.

I've tried plenty of task managers and project boards over the years. The catch is always the same. Keeping the board updated turns into another chore, and one busy week knocks you right off it. I didn't want another system to manage. I wanted something that manages the system.

What a normal day looks like now

My weekdays feel pretty different than they did a couple of months ago, mostly because the admin pile doesn't sit around waiting until 5 PM.

It starts with a morning brief. Duet pulls from my task list and calendar, lays out my day from 10 to 5, and flags what's urgent. I don't start the morning clicking through five tabs trying to remember what slipped yesterday.

Then comes the drive in. I get on a call with Duet, phone in the cupholder, and we knock out open decisions. I talk, it asks me one decision at a time, I say yes or no (maybe add a quick note if it needs context), and it asks for the next one. That 15-minute ride clears stuff that used to sit in my head for days.

While I'm working, Duet sorts my inbox and meeting notes into tracked tasks a few times a day automatically. I'm not sitting down in the evening copying action items out of email just so I don't forget them.

Midday I take a gym break, handle the work that actually needs me, and around 4 PM I do a replies round. Client replies are already drafted and waiting. I read them over, tweak a line if needed, and tap send on the ones I like.

Between those blocks, it stays out of my way. It only checks in through the day when something actually needs me.

It didn't start out that clean, though. Early on, Duet threw way too much at me at once. Long messages, several questions stacked together, way more text than I wanted to sort through midday. I told it straight up that it was too much, and it changed. Short answers, one ask at a time. That single correction made the whole setup usable.

Real examples from my agency

Daily routines sound nice on paper, so here's what Duet has actually handled for me over the last month.

Keeping a second brain updated

Emails and meeting notes become tasks with an owner, next step, client, and project attached. Early on, Duet went through every open task on my board and set a priority on each one so the urgent stuff stopped hiding under small chores.

When I realized my board had a bunch of "someday" ideas cluttering up the week, I asked for an "Icebox" status. It added the status and moved the right tasks in. It also works alongside my other AI agents (you can see the rest of the tools I actually use) and helps decide who does what, so I'm not spending half my morning playing traffic cop.

Client communication

Most client replies get drafted in my voice and sit on a card until I hit send. When it was time to send monthly results emails for a batch of clients, Duet wrote those drafts too, and included a note reminding clients that calls straight from Google aren't counted in that report. That kind of context keeps everyone on the same page about what the numbers show.

It also watches threads I don't want buried. When I was waiting on one client's reply about a website handoff, Duet kept an eye on the inbox for that message so I could stay focused on other work.

Google Ads checks

This is where I saw Duet start helping with real technical work alongside the inbox stuff.

For a body shop client, it pulled together a plain-English 30-day report on their search ads so you could read what was happening without digging through 10 screens. On a van repair campaign, it caught that ads were paying for "for sale" and "price" searches (people shopping for vans, not looking to fix one) and built a negative keyword list to stop paying for those clicks.

Before Thanksgiving, it helped pause two clients' ads over the holiday and set a reminder to turn them back on afterward.

It also handled a mistake the right way. It flagged a few keywords to block that were already blocked in the account, and when that came up, it admitted the miss straight up instead of making excuses.

The biggest catch happened while we were planning after-hours ad campaigns. Looking into the account turned up that one client's website form leads weren't being tracked at all. Catching a blind spot like that before pushing more ad traffic to a site saves a lot of headaches.

Google Business Profile posts

On the local SEO side, I had job photos sitting in Google Drive that needed to become a profile post (I've written before about why real job photos beat AI flyers). Duet wrote the draft post and checked both the photo date and the local weather so a snow post didn't go up on a sunny day.

Small detail, right? A real person catches that without thinking, and most automated posting tools never would.

Running my directory site

Outside of client work, I run EuroRepairHQ, a directory strictly for European auto repair specialists. When a shop submits a claim to get listed, Duet reads that shop's own website and checks it against my European-specialists-only rule.

If a shop doesn't fit, it drafts a custom rejection email explaining why, and sends it only after my okay. It also built a call sheet of shops with no website that already get traffic on my directory. That turns traffic I already have into a practical outreach list.

Bigger website builds

I've been moving one of my sites off WordPress without losing the pages that already show up in search. Duet helps plan the move, starts the coding jobs, and brings back preview links for me to review. Nothing merges or goes live without me.

It also ran keyword research and put together a first draft of my AI consulting page. Instead of guessing at how I work or making up claims, it left questions in the draft for me to answer. I'd way rather fill in five quick blanks than hunt down two invented sentences.

When tools hit a wall

One small moment I liked: a website blocked Duet's cloud browser while it was running a check. Rather than stopping and handing the errand back to me, it switched to Chrome on my computer and finished the check there.

The guardrails I keep on it

If handing this much admin work to software sounds a little nerve-wracking, I get it. I wouldn't run my business on autopilot either.

My main rule is simple: Duet drafts, I send. Emails, merges, publishing, payments, and anything public all wait for my yes. If my name or a client's name is going on it, my eyes go on it first.

It also remembers my rules and corrections once I give them. Early on I told it never to send my chats to anyone, and it doesn't. You shouldn't have to remind your tools of your boundaries every Monday morning.

Just as important, it tells me when it isn't sure, and where a fact came from. I can work with "here's what I checked, and here's what I couldn't confirm." Software that guesses with total confidence isn't useful to me.

I treat it like a chief of staff. It gets the details lined up and puts the real decisions on my desk, and I still make the calls.

Features I haven't tried yet

Full disclosure, I'm only using a slice of what Duet offers (you can check Anthropic's getting started guide for Duet if you want the full rundown). Since I'd rather talk about what I've actually tested than pretend I've mastered the whole thing, here's what I still haven't turned on.

You can have it learn your writing style from emails you've already sent. I never set that up, so my drafts so far come mostly from my instructions and feedback.

There are also "no need to ask" rules for routine messages you write yourself, plus the option to hand off more categories of tasks so they run without waiting. Right now I only have one "never" rule set up, and every kind of task is still on "draft it for me."

Payments and logins aren't wired up either. Duet can pay through a Stripe Link or Shop Pay wallet with my approval on every purchase, and sign into accounts with 1Password while I approve each login on my phone. Neither one is connected yet.

On the business side, it has places and maps tools for searching local businesses, which could turn into a handy prospecting tool. It also comes with small business skills for overdue invoices, a cash-flow snapshot, sorting leads, and answering reviews.

And for anyone wondering if it's strictly a work tool, it can also handle errands like food orders, rides, and restaurant bookings.

What this means for your business

You might be thinking, okay, that works for a marketing guy who lives on a laptop, what does it actually do for a trade business?

Once you strip out the industry labels, the admin pile looks pretty similar across almost every local shop I talk to.

If you run a plumbing company, your morning drive could clear follow-ups on yesterday's quotes and turn voicemails into tracked jobs before your first service call. If you own an auto shop, it could turn bay photos into a Google Business Profile post, catch wasted ad clicks, and draft replies to estimate requests while you're dealing with cars on the lift. If you run a landscaping crew, it could keep customer emails sorted during spring rush so bids and schedule changes stop falling through the cracks.

If you want to test something like this without biting off too much at once, start with three easy wins:

  1. Inbox triage that turns loose emails and call notes into one clean task list.
  2. Drafted customer replies waiting for you to review and send before you head home.
  3. A short daily brief built from your calendar and open tasks so you know what's urgent before the phone starts ringing.

Plan for a real setup period. Connecting your apps and teaching it your rules isn't a five-minute trick. Expect a few weeks of small corrections, pretty much like showing a new office person how you like things handled.

And this doesn't replace good people. Your techs, your office staff, and your own judgment are the business. Tools like Duet just take the repetitive busywork off your desk so you can spend your time on the work only you can do.
